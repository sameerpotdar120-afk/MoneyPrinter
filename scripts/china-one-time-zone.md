# Why China Has Only ONE Time Zone (But Russia Has 11)

Target length: 7 minutes. Faceless narration over maps and simple illustrations.

Thumbnail idea: a map of China filled in one solid color, a sun just on the horizon in the far west, a clock showing 10:00, and the text "10 AM SUNRISE?"

---

## 0:00 Hook

[ON SCREEN: Dark winter map of far-western China. A clock in the corner reads 9:59, then 10:00. The sun only now appears on the horizon.]

In December, you could wait until after 10 in the morning to see the sun rise in China. Why would a country choose that?

[ON SCREEN: Side by side. Continental US split into 4 colored bands. China, about the same width, filled in one color.]

Because it did choose it. China is about as wide as the continental United States. The US splits that width into four time zones. China uses one.

## 0:20 The turn

[ON SCREEN: Three quick cards: "Beijing's clock" / "Life 3,000 km west" / "The country with the MOST time zones ???"]

In the next seven minutes: why every clock in China runs on Beijing's time, what that does to people living more than 3,000 kilometers west of the capital, and why China isn't even the strangest case. One country has more time zones than Russia, and it's probably not the one you'd guess.

## 0:41 Beat 1: How China ended up on one clock

[ON SCREEN: Map of China split into five vertical zones, labeled "Before 1949". The lines fade out one by one until the whole map is one color: "Beijing Time, UTC+8".]

China didn't always work this way. Before 1949, the country was divided into five time zones. Then the Communist Party took power, and the new government put the entire country on a single clock: Beijing time, eight hours ahead of Greenwich.

[ON SCREEN: Two clocks, Shanghai and the Afghan border, both showing 8:00. A line between them: "4,000+ km".]

The usual explanation is unity. One country, one capital, one time. So when it's 8 in the morning in Shanghai, it's also 8 in the morning on the border with Afghanistan, more than 4,000 kilometers away. And today there's no daylight saving time either. The clock never moves.

## 1:17 Beat 2: Life in the far west

[ON SCREEN: Zoom from Beijing west to Kashgar, in Xinjiang. A sun arc over Kashgar with its highest point marked "almost 3:00 PM".]

Here's what that looks like on the ground. Kashgar is a city in Xinjiang, China's far western region. The sun there runs so far behind Beijing's clock that solar noon, the moment the sun is highest in the sky, comes at almost 3 in the afternoon. In December, sunrise doesn't come until after 10.

[ON SCREEN: A shop door with opening hours shifted later. Then two clocks side by side, "Beijing time" and "Xinjiang time", two hours apart.]

So daily life bends around it. Schools, shops and offices in Xinjiang often start later in the day. And many people there, especially in the Uyghur community, keep their own unofficial clock, called Xinjiang time, two hours behind Beijing.

[ON SCREEN: Two simple characters. One asks "What time is it?" The other answers "Beijing time or Xinjiang time?"]

Which means that in a city like Ürümqi, you can ask someone what time it is and get a question back: Beijing time, or Xinjiang time? Two clocks, one city, and both of them are right.

## 2:08 Beat 3: The strangest border on Earth

[ON SCREEN: Zoom into the Wakhan Corridor, a thin strip of Afghanistan touching China high in the Pamir mountains. Labels: "China UTC+8" | "Afghanistan UTC+4:30".]

It gets stranger at the edges. China's border with Afghanistan is a short, thin line high up in the Pamir mountains. On the Chinese side, it's Beijing time, eight hours ahead of Greenwich. On the Afghan side, it's four and a half.

[ON SCREEN: A watch spinning backward. Text: "-3.5 HOURS".]

Step across that line and your watch goes back three and a half hours. That's the biggest time jump between any two neighboring countries in the world. There's no regular crossing open to travelers up there, so almost nobody ever experiences it. But on the map, it's real.

## 2:44 Beat 4: Russia made the opposite choice, then tried China's

[ON SCREEN: Russia split into 11 colored bands, from Kaliningrad "UTC+2" to Kamchatka "UTC+12".]

Now look north. Russia is even wider than China, and it went the other way: eleven time zones, from Kaliningrad in the west, two hours ahead of Greenwich, to Kamchatka in the far east, twelve hours ahead.

[ON SCREEN: "2010" stamp. Two of the bands merge, leaving 9. Then "2014" stamp, and the bands split back to 11.]

But Russia did flirt with China's idea. In 2010, President Dmitry Medvedev cut the number of zones from eleven to nine, arguing the huge country would be easier to run with fewer of them. It didn't last. After years of complaints about dark winter mornings, Russia went back to eleven zones in 2014.

## 3:20 Beat 5: Who actually decides what time it is?

[ON SCREEN: An 1880s American town map, every town with a slightly different clock face.]

So who decides what time it is? Not the sun. Before the 1880s, almost every town in America set its clocks by its own local noon. Every town was a few minutes off from the next one, and that was fine, until trains.

[ON SCREEN: Old-style railroad map. Date stamp: "November 18, 1883". The map snaps into four bands.]

You can't run a train timetable when every station has its own time. So on November 18, 1883, the American railroads switched to standard time zones on their own. The US government didn't make it law until 1918.

[ON SCREEN: World map. A line drops through Greenwich, London. Stamp: "Washington, 1884". France highlighted with a "?".]

In 1884, delegates met in Washington and picked Greenwich, in London, as the line every clock on Earth would count from. France didn't vote for it. It kept running on Paris time until 1911.

## 4:06 Beat 6: Spain is in the wrong time zone

[ON SCREEN: Europe map. A vertical line runs through Britain, Portugal and Spain. Then Spain's color jumps to match Germany's. Stamp: "1940".]

Once you see that time zones are political, you start seeing it everywhere. Look at Spain. On the map, it lines up with Britain and Portugal. Its clocks match Germany's. In 1940, Francisco Franco moved Spain's clocks forward an hour to line up with Nazi Germany, and Spain never moved them back.

[ON SCREEN: A Spanish dinner table. A wall clock reads 9:00, and a sun outside sits where it would at about 7.]

Some historians argue that's part of why Spaniards eat dinner so late. On a summer evening in Madrid, the clock might say 9, but the sun says it's closer to 7.

## 4:40 Beat 7: India's quiet workaround, and Nepal's 15 minutes

[ON SCREEN: Map of India in one color, labeled "UTC+5:30". The northeast highlighted, with a sun rising early.]

China isn't the only giant on one clock. India, the most populous country on Earth, also runs on a single time zone, set half an hour off the usual grid at five and a half hours ahead of Greenwich. In the far northeast, that means the sun comes up very early and sets very early.

[ON SCREEN: A tea garden in Assam. A clock labeled "Tea garden time" set one hour ahead of a clock labeled "IST".]

So some tea gardens in Assam have kept their own clock since British rule: "tea garden time", one hour ahead of the rest of India, so work can start with the daylight.

[ON SCREEN: Map zooms to Nepal. Clock: "UTC+5:45". Text: "+15 minutes vs India".]

And right next door, Nepal set its clock fifteen minutes ahead of India's, at five hours and forty-five minutes, one of the very few countries in the world on a quarter-hour time zone.

## 5:28 Beat 8: The payoff, the country with the most time zones

[ON SCREEN: Text card: "Most time zones in the world?" Russia's map flashes, then gets crossed out.]

Which brings us back to the promise from the start. The country with the most time zones in the world isn't Russia, and it isn't the United States. It's France.

[ON SCREEN: World map. Mainland France lights up, then dots light up across the globe: French Guiana, Martinique, Réunion, New Caledonia, French Polynesia. Counter ticks up to 12.]

Mainland France fits inside a single time zone. But count its overseas territories, from French Guiana in South America to Réunion in the Indian Ocean to French Polynesia in the Pacific, and France covers twelve time zones. That's more than any other country on Earth.

## 5:58 Bonus beat: The country that deleted a day

[ON SCREEN: A calendar for December 2011 on Samoa. Thursday the 29th, then Friday the 30th gets torn off and thrown away, then Saturday the 31st.]

And if a government can move the clock, it can move the calendar too. In 2011, Samoa moved itself to the other side of the International Date Line to line up with its biggest trading partners, Australia and New Zealand. Thursday, December 29th was followed by Saturday, December 31st. In Samoa, Friday the 30th never happened.

## 6:20 Answer and close

[ON SCREEN: Back to the opening shot. The China map in one color, the late sunrise over Kashgar.]

So here's the answer to the question we started with. China has one time zone because in 1949, a new government decided the whole country should run on the capital's clock. It's not about the sun, and it's not about science. Time zones are lines drawn by governments, railroads and dictators. The sun just has to deal with it.

[ON SCREEN: Text card: "When is solar noon where YOU live?" with a comment icon.]

So look up when solar noon is in your city. It's probably not 12 o'clock. Tell me in the comments how far off yours is.

## Facts to double-check before you record

I kept to well-documented facts, but check these against a second source before publishing:

- China used five time zones before 1949 and one (UTC+8) after.
- Kashgar: solar noon around 2:55 PM Beijing time; December sunrise around 10:10 AM. I calculated these from Kashgar's coordinates, so confirm them on a sunrise site such as timeanddate.com.
- China–Afghanistan: UTC+8 vs UTC+4:30, a 3.5-hour difference, the largest between neighboring countries.
- Russia: 11 zones (UTC+2 to UTC+12), cut to 9 in 2010 under Medvedev, back to 11 in 2014.
- US railroads adopted standard time on November 18, 1883. The Standard Time Act was passed in 1918.
- International Meridian Conference, Washington, 1884. France didn't vote for Greenwich and used Paris time until 1911.
- Spain moved to Central European Time in 1940 under Franco.
- France spans 12 time zones (13 if you count its Antarctic claim).
- India runs on one zone (UTC+5:30). Some Assam tea gardens keep "tea garden time" (Chaibagan time), one hour ahead.
- Nepal is on UTC+5:45, 15 minutes ahead of India.
- Samoa skipped Friday, December 30, 2011.
